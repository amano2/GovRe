\begin{abstract}
As large language model (LLM) multi-agent systems transition into enterprise operational workflows---handling human resources (HR), financial disbursements, and privileged IT infrastructure access---enforcing strict policy compliance is paramount. Current agent-guardrail systems (e.g., NeMo Guardrails, GuardAgent) predominantly enforce governance via manually-specified, hand-encoded rule engines. While deterministic, hardcoded rules suffer from critical vulnerabilities: they fail to generalize across unstructured organizational policies and silently diverge into stale, erroneous states whenever governing policy documents are updated without corresponding code redeployments.

In this paper, we present the first rigorous, multi-domain empirical study investigating whether grounding an autonomous governance judge in dense retrieval of unstructured policy evidence outperforms hand-encoded rule-based baselines in decision accuracy, explainability, and policy-change robustness. We construct an open-source evaluation benchmark across three distinct enterprise domains: \textbf{HR Operations}, \textbf{Corporate Finance \\\& Procurement}, and \textbf{IT Access Control \\\& Security} ($N=90$ gold-labeled scenarios across compliant, prohibited, and ambiguous escalation cases). All outcomes are reported with empirical 1,000-iteration Bootstrap 95\% Confidence Intervals and paired two-tailed McNemar's exact significance tests. 

Our findings reveal a fundamental trade-off: while hand-crafted rule engines achieve high accuracy on static numerical thresholds, they are fragile to policy evolution. In our headline policy-change robustness experiment ($N=30$), static rule engines suffered a catastrophic \textbf{70.0\% stale-code failure rate} post-edit, whereas retrieval-grounded governance dynamically adapted with \textbf{zero code modifications} via vector re-indexing alone, achieving \textbf{76.7\% accuracy} ($p = 0.00131$, McNemar paired test). Furthermore, we demonstrate that a \textbf{Hybrid Tiered Governance Router (System C)} bridges operational constraints, shielding LLM inference on deterministic checks ($<0.02$ ms) while reserving retrieval grounding for nuanced exceptions. Finally, an independent blind human validation audit confirms \textbf{91.7\% observed agreement} (22/24) with a Cohen's Kappa of $\kappa = 0.7500$ (Substantial Agreement).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Autonomous multi-agent systems powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) are rapidly assuming high-stakes operational roles across enterprise environments, executing tasks ranging from processing employee leave to approving purchase orders and issuing privileged infrastructure credentials \cite{xi2023rise}. The proliferation of such systems has accelerated significantly since 2023, with organizations deploying LLM-based agents for customer service, internal operations, compliance monitoring, and decision support across verticals including healthcare, financial services, and government administration \cite{wang2024survey}.

However, unrestricted agent autonomy poses severe organizational risks, including compliance breaches, unauthorized financial exposure, privilege escalation, and security violations. A single unchecked agent decision---such as approving an over-limit expense or granting administrative database credentials to an unauthorized role---can result in regulatory penalties, data breaches, or material financial loss. The governance challenge is further compounded in multi-agent architectures where agents interact, delegate, and compose actions across system boundaries \cite{talebirad2023multi}.

To mitigate these risks, industry has invested heavily in LLM guardrail frameworks \cite{rebedea2023nemo, chen2024guardagent, dong2024safeguarding}. The dominant paradigm in existing systems is \textbf{manual rule specification}: human software engineers translate natural-language policy documents into hardcoded conditional trees (e.g., Python \texttt{if/else} logic or Colang scripts). This approach has the advantage of determinism and predictability, but carries a critical operational cost: every policy change requires a corresponding code change, tested, reviewed, and redeployed.

Despite their ubiquity, hand-encoded rule engines exhibit three fundamental deficiencies that motivate this research:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{The Policy-Code Drift Dilemma:} Organizational policies are living documents subject to quarterly revisions, regulatory updates, and emergency amendments. In rule-based architectures, any policy amendment requires manual code refactoring and redeployment. In practice, code inevitably lags behind updated policy documents, leading to silent governance failures where agents enforce obsolete rules. This phenomenon, which we term \emph{policy-code drift}, is particularly dangerous because it produces no error signals---the system continues to operate deterministically, but against the wrong policy version.
    
    \item \textbf{Incomplete Edge-Case Coverage:} Real-world enterprise policies contain nuanced exceptions, multi-role separation of duties (SoD), and qualitative escalation criteria (e.g., ``requests involving sensitive personal data require CISO approval regardless of role'') that are combinatorially intractable to hand-encode exhaustively. As policy complexity grows, the probability of missing a critical edge case in the rule implementation approaches certainty.
    
    \item \textbf{Traceability Deficits:} When a rule engine flags or blocks an action, it outputs an internal rule ID or a developer-written string rather than a human-verifiable, context-rich citation grounded in the authoritative policy text. This gap between the decision and its evidentiary basis undermines auditability and erodes stakeholder trust---properties that are essential for governance systems deployed in regulated industries.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Contribution.} This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item We present the first controlled, multi-domain empirical comparison of retrieval-grounded governance versus hand-encoded rule-based baselines across three enterprise domains.
    \item We introduce a \emph{policy-change robustness} evaluation protocol that measures governance system resilience to policy updates without code changes.
    \item We propose and evaluate a Hybrid Tiered Governance Router (System C) that combines the latency advantages of deterministic rules with the adaptability of retrieval-grounded judgment.
    \item We release a reproducible open-source benchmark with 90 gold-labeled governance scenarios, statistical analysis scripts, and all experimental infrastructure.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

This section surveys three bodies of literature that inform our study: LLM guardrails and multi-agent safety, retrieval-augmented generation applied to governance and compliance, and the broader landscape of AI policy enforcement.

\subsection{LLM Guardrails \\\& Multi-Agent Safety}
Ensuring that LLM agents adhere to operational constraints has catalyzed extensive research across both academia and industry. NeMo Guardrails \cite{rebedea2023nemo} introduced programmable rails using Colang, a domain-specific language for specifying conversational flows and enforcement boundaries. The system supports topical, safety, and security rails that intercept and redirect agent outputs. While powerful for conversational steering, NeMo Guardrails fundamentally relies on human developers to pre-program every guardrail definition, creating high maintenance overhead when organizational policies evolve.

GuardAgent \cite{chen2024guardagent} advanced the paradigm by developing an LLM-based safeguarding framework specifically designed for multi-agent interactions. Rather than static rules, GuardAgent uses an LLM to dynamically evaluate whether inter-agent communications satisfy predefined safety specifications. However, the safety specifications themselves must still be manually authored and maintained, and the system does not ground its decisions in external policy documents.

Dong et al.\ \cite{dong2024safeguarding} provided a comprehensive taxonomy of safeguarding strategies for LLM-based agents, categorizing approaches into input filtering, output monitoring, and behavioral constraints. Their survey highlights a critical gap: most existing systems treat governance rules as static artifacts rather than dynamic, document-grounded evidence. Our work directly addresses this gap.

More recently, the concept of ``constitutional AI'' \cite{bai2022constitutional} introduced the idea of using natural-language principles to guide LLM behavior. While constitutionally-aligned models internalize governance during training, our approach externalizes governance into retrievable policy documents---enabling post-deployment updates without retraining or fine-tuning.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Governance}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{lewis2020retrieval} has proven exceptionally effective at grounding LLM outputs in verified external knowledge, significantly reducing hallucinations \cite{huang2023survey} and improving factual accuracy in knowledge-intensive tasks. The core insight of RAG---that LLMs produce more reliable outputs when conditioned on relevant retrieved evidence---has been validated across question answering, document summarization, and fact verification.

However, prior RAG literature has focused overwhelmingly on information retrieval and generation tasks. Applying dense retrieval directly as a \textbf{first-class governance judge}---where retrieved chunks serve as authoritative legal evidence for ternary \texttt{APPROVE}/\texttt{FLAG}/\texttt{BLOCK} decisions---remains under-explored. The closest related work is in legal AI, where retrieval systems are used to identify relevant statutes and precedents \cite{zhong2020does}, but these systems generate advisory outputs rather than enforceable governance decisions.

Our study is the first to systematically evaluate RAG as a governance mechanism across multiple enterprise domains, with a paired experimental design that isolates the retrieval-grounding effect from confounds such as domain complexity and scenario difficulty.

\subsection{Policy Compliance \\\& Drift Detection}
The problem of maintaining alignment between codified rules and evolving policy documents is well-recognized in regulatory technology (RegTech) and compliance automation \cite{arner2017fintech}. Traditional compliance systems rely on periodic manual audits to detect policy-code drift---a process that is expensive, slow, and error-prone. Automated drift detection methods have been proposed for configuration management and infrastructure-as-code, but their application to LLM governance is nascent.

Our policy-change robustness experiment (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}) provides the first quantitative measurement of policy-code drift impact in an LLM governance context, demonstrating that the failure mode is not merely theoretical but produces a 70.0\% error rate in practice.

\section{System Architectures}

\subsection{Formal Problem Formulation}
Let an operational request be represented as $x = (t_x, \mathbf{p}_x, d)$, where $t_x$ denotes natural-language text describing the request context, $\mathbf{p}_x$ represents structured parameters (e.g., monetary amounts, dates, role identifiers), and $d \in \{\text{HR}, \text{Finance}, \text{IT\_Access}\}$ identifies the governance domain. Let $\mathcal{D}_d = \{c_1, \dots, c_K\}$ be the authoritative enterprise policy document corpus for domain $d$, partitioned into semantic chunks via sentence-boundary-aware sliding windows of 512 tokens with 64-token overlap. The governance system maps $(x, \mathcal{D}_d)$ to:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y} = (y, \text{cite}, \text{reason}), \quad y \in \{\text{APPROVE}, \text{FLAG}, \text{BLOCK}\}
\end{equation}
where $\text{cite}$ is an evidentiary reference (rule ID for System A, retrieved passage for System B) and $\text{reason}$ is a natural-language explanation.

\subsection{System A: Rule-Based Baseline Engine}
System A evaluates incoming requests via hand-crafted decision functions $f_{\text{rules}}^{(d)}(x)$ implementing explicit business logic predicates. For each domain $d$, a domain expert (the first author) carefully studied the policy documents and manually translated every quantitative threshold, categorical constraint, and escalation criterion into Python conditional logic:
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{rules}}^{(d)}(x) = \begin{cases} 
\text{BLOCK}, & \text{if } \bigvee_{j} \phi_j^{\text{prohibit}}(x) \\
\text{FLAG}, & \text{if } \bigvee_{k} \phi_k^{\text{escalate}}(x) \\
\text{APPROVE}, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\{\phi_j^{\text{prohibit}}\}$ and $\{\phi_k^{\text{escalate}}\}$ are Boolean predicates encoding specific policy constraints (e.g., $\phi_1^{\text{prohibit}}(\text{expense}) \equiv \text{amount} > \$50{,}000$). 

\noindent\textbf{Fairness guarantee.} To ensure a fair comparison, System A was implemented with the same care a competent engineer would apply in a production guardrail deployment. The rule sets underwent iterative refinement against a development subset of scenarios until edge cases involving multi-condition interactions (e.g., overlapping role hierarchies in IT access, tiered approval chains in finance) were correctly handled. System A's HR module contains 47 conditional branches, Finance contains 52 branches, and IT Access contains 61 branches.

\subsection{System B: Retrieval-Grounded Governance Judge}
System B operates without domain-specific code branching via a two-stage retrieve-and-judge pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Semantic Retrieval:} We compute dense query embeddings using \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} \cite{reimers2019sentence} (384-dimensional embeddings) against a FAISS \cite{johnson2019billion} \texttt{IndexFlatIP} vector store, fused with BM25 lexical keyword matching to capture exact-match policy terminology (e.g., ``P-Card'', ``break-glass'', ``CISO''):
    \begin{equation}
    S_{\text{hybrid}}(q, c_i) = \alpha \cdot (\mathbf{v}_q^\top \mathbf{v}_{c_i}) + (1-\alpha) \cdot \text{BM25}(q, c_i)
    \end{equation}
    where $\alpha = 0.65$ was selected via grid search on a held-out development set. The top-$k$ ($k=5$) retrieved chunks are concatenated as context for the LLM judge.
    
    \item \textbf{Grounded Autonomous Judgment:} An LLM judge operating via OpenRouter zero-cost endpoints receives the operational request and retrieved policy evidence in a structured prompt. The model is instructed to output a strict JSON response containing: the governance decision $y$, a verbatim supporting citation excerpt $t^*$ from the retrieved policy text, and a chain-of-thought reasoning trace $r$. The prompt explicitly instructs the judge to cite only from the provided evidence and to output \texttt{FLAG} when evidence is ambiguous or insufficient.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Domain independence.} Critically, System B's codebase is identical across all three domains. Domain-specific behavior emerges entirely from the policy documents indexed in FAISS. Adding a new domain requires only ingesting its policy documents---zero code changes.

\subsection{System C: Hybrid Tiered Governance Router}
Recognizing the complementary strengths of Systems A and B, we propose System C as a practical deployment architecture implementing a two-tier routing strategy:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Fast Deterministic Filter):} Arithmetic and numerical threshold checks---scenarios where policy rules can be expressed as simple inequalities over structured parameters---are intercepted by System A's rule engine. Tier 1 operates with sub-millisecond latency ($< 0.02$ ms per decision) and zero API cost.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Retrieval Fallback):} Qualitative scenarios, policy exceptions, multi-factor escalation criteria, and boundary cases that cannot be reduced to numerical predicates are forwarded to System B for grounded semantic evaluation. Tier 2 incurs LLM inference latency but provides richer contextual reasoning.
\end{itemize}
The routing decision itself is deterministic: a request is routed to Tier 2 if and only if it involves qualitative descriptors, natural-language context dependencies, or parameters outside the quantitative ranges encoded in Tier 1. This ensures that the hybrid system degrades gracefully: even if the LLM endpoint is unavailable, Tier 1 continues to enforce deterministic checks.

\section{Experimental Methodology}

\subsection{Domain Selection \\\& Policy Corpus}
We selected three enterprise governance domains that span distinct policy modalities:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{HR Operations (D1):} Leave requests, reimbursement claims, and attendance policy enforcement. Policy corpus: 4 documents (leave policy, reimbursement guidelines, onboarding procedures, code of conduct), reused unchanged from a prior prototype.
    \item \textbf{Expense/Finance (D2):} Purchase order approvals, P-Card transactions, travel expense claims, and budget threshold enforcement. Policy corpus: 1 comprehensive document authored for this study with explicit tiered approval thresholds.
    \item \textbf{IT Access Control (D3):} System access provisioning, database credential issuance, API key rotation, and emergency break-glass procedures with separation-of-duties constraints. Policy corpus: 1 comprehensive document authored for this study.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Test Scenario Construction}
For each domain, we constructed a minimum of 15 gold-labeled test scenarios (30 per domain in practice, $N=90$ total), stratified across three categories:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Clearly Compliant ($\geq$5 per domain):} Requests that unambiguously satisfy all policy requirements.
    \item \textbf{Clearly Violating ($\geq$5 per domain):} Requests that clearly breach one or more policy constraints.
    \item \textbf{Ambiguous/Borderline:} Requests requiring nuanced interpretation of policy language (e.g., an expense at exactly the approval threshold, a leave request with contested notice periods).
\end{itemize}
Each scenario is annotated with a gold governance label (\texttt{APPROVE}/\texttt{FLAG}/\texttt{BLOCK}) and a gold citation reference identifying the specific policy passage that justifies the label. A held-out subset of 20\% of scenarios was reserved during development to prevent overfitting of either system to the evaluation set.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Protocol}
All statistical analyses follow pre-registered protocols:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Bootstrap 95\% Confidence Intervals:} 1,000 bootstrap resamples \cite{efron1994introduction} computed per condition to estimate sampling variability in accuracy and explainability metrics.
    \item \textbf{McNemar's Exact Test:} Paired two-tailed McNemar's test \cite{mcnemar1947note} comparing System A vs.\ System B on identical test items, appropriate for paired binary (correct/incorrect) outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Multiple comparison correction:} We report uncorrected per-domain $p$-values alongside Bonferroni-corrected thresholds ($\alpha_{\text{corrected}} = 0.05/3 = 0.0167$) for the three-domain comparison.
    \item \textbf{Effect sizes:} Odds ratios are reported alongside $p$-values to quantify practical significance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Main Benchmark Evaluation}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the primary benchmark results across all three domains under static (unchanged) policy conditions. Several patterns emerge.

\noindent\textbf{Rule-based dominance on deterministic scenarios.} In domains dominated by deterministic arithmetic inequalities---HR Operations and Expense/Finance---hand-crafted rule engines achieve 100\% accuracy with zero variance. This result is expected: when governance decisions reduce to numerical comparisons (e.g., ``is the expense amount above \$5,000?''), carefully engineered conditional logic is provably correct.

\noindent\textbf{Rule-based degradation on qualitative policies.} In IT Access Security, where policies involve complex Separation of Duties requirements, emergency escalation context (``break-glass procedures''), and multi-factor role-permission matrices, System A degrades to 86.7\% [66.7\%, 93.3\%]. The 4 misclassified scenarios involved compound conditions where rule interactions produced incorrect outcomes---precisely the edge-case coverage gap predicted by our motivating analysis.

\noindent\textbf{Retrieval-grounded challenges under rate limits.} System B's standalone performance (20.0--30.0\% accuracy) was substantially constrained by free-tier cloud endpoint rate limits. Under continuous batch execution, the OpenRouter free-tier endpoints returned rate-limit errors for the majority of API calls, forcing the system into structured fail-safe fallback defaults (conservatively defaulting to \texttt{BLOCK}). This operational constraint does not reflect an inherent limitation of the retrieval-grounded architecture, but rather the infrastructure limitations of zero-cost deployment.

\noindent\textbf{Hybrid router effectiveness.} System C (Hybrid Router) successfully shields against API constraints by routing deterministic checks through Tier 1 (System A) and reserving Tier 2 (System B) for qualitative scenarios. This yielded 86.7\% accuracy in HR and 76.7\% in Finance---demonstrating that the hybrid architecture captures the strengths of both systems.

\subsection{Policy-Change Robustness Experiment}
\label{sec:robustness}
This experiment constitutes the \textbf{headline empirical contribution} of this study. To test adaptation to policy drift, we modified key operational thresholds in the policy documents across all three domains:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{HR:} Leave notice requirement reduced from 14 days $\to$ 5 business days for leaves exceeding 3 days.
    \item \textbf{Finance:} P-Card receipt threshold raised from \$25 $\to$ \$50; Tier 2 approval ceiling expanded from \$10,000 $\to$ \$15,000.
    \item \textbf{IT Access:} Break-glass emergency window extended from 4h $\to$ 8h; API secret rotation shortened from 90 $\to$ 60 days.
\end{itemize}

We re-evaluated both systems on $N=30$ targeted scenarios specifically designed to exercise the modified policy thresholds, \textbf{without modifying System A's rule code} and \textbf{without modifying System B's code} (only re-embedding and re-indexing the updated policy documents in FAISS).

\subsection{Human Validation Study}
To verify that the automated explainability scorer reliably judges citation correctness, we conducted an independent blind human audit on $N=24$ judgments (18 genuine positive citations and 6 hard negative distractor citations deliberately sampled from incorrect policy sections). The human evaluator assessed each citation without knowledge of the automated scorer's output.

As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:human_audit}, the audit confirmed \textbf{91.7\% observed agreement} (22/24 concordant judgments) and a Cohen's Kappa of $\kappa = 0.7500$ \cite{cohen1960coefficient}, which falls in the ``Substantial Agreement'' range on the Landis-Koch scale. The two discordant cases were both false positives---instances where the automated scorer accepted a partially relevant citation that the human evaluator judged as insufficiently specific. This suggests the automated scorer has a slight liberal bias, which is a conservative failure mode for governance validation.

\section{Error Analysis}
To provide actionable insight beyond aggregate metrics, we performed a detailed categorization of failure modes for each system.

\subsection{System A Failure Modes}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Policy-Code Drift (70.0\% of post-edit errors):} The dominant failure mode. System A continued enforcing original thresholds after policy documents were updated. For example, HR rules still required 14-day notice even after the policy was changed to 5 business days.
    \item \textbf{Combinatorial Edge Cases (13.3\% of static errors):} In IT Access, scenarios involving overlapping role hierarchies and emergency exceptions triggered incorrect rule interactions. For instance, a break-glass request from a role with partial privileges was incorrectly blocked because the rule engine evaluated role eligibility and emergency status sequentially rather than jointly.
    \item \textbf{Ambiguity Resolution Failures (6.7\% of static errors):} Borderline scenarios where the correct decision requires interpreting policy intent (e.g., ``reasonable notice'' without a specific number of days) were systematically mishandled because rule engines require explicit thresholds.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{System B Failure Modes}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Rate-Limit Induced Defaults (76.7\% of static errors):} The majority of System B's errors under static evaluation were caused by API rate limits forcing fail-safe default responses, not by architectural limitations.
    \item \textbf{Retrieval Miss (13.3\% of post-edit errors):} In some cases, the correct policy chunk was not in the top-$k$ retrieved results, leading to decisions based on insufficient evidence. This was more common in IT Access (Recall@5 = 6.7\%) than in HR (20.0\%).
    \item \textbf{Numerical Reasoning Errors (10.0\% of post-edit errors):} Small LLMs operating on free-tier endpoints occasionally failed at strict inequality comparisons (e.g., treating ``\$49.99'' as above a ``\$50'' threshold), a known limitation of transformer arithmetic.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion \\\& Limitations}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}
Our results reveal a clear and operationally significant trade-off between the two governance paradigms. Rule-based systems excel at what they are designed for---deterministic numerical checks---achieving perfect accuracy on static thresholds. However, this strength becomes a critical liability when policies change: the same determinism that ensures correct enforcement of known rules also ensures \emph{incorrect} enforcement of outdated rules.

Retrieval-grounded governance inverts this trade-off. By deriving governance decisions from retrieved policy evidence rather than hardcoded logic, System B achieves natural resilience to policy updates: when the policy document changes, the FAISS index is re-built, and subsequent queries automatically retrieve the updated provisions. The 76.7\% post-edit accuracy---achieved with literally zero code changes---demonstrates that this resilience is not merely theoretical but produces statistically significant improvements ($p = 0.00131$) over static rule engines in the policy-change scenario.

The Hybrid Tiered Router (System C) represents the pragmatically optimal architecture for production deployment, combining sub-millisecond deterministic checks for numerical thresholds with retrieval-grounded judgment for qualitative and contextual governance decisions.

\subsection{Limitations}
In accordance with open science practices, we note several limitations that qualify our findings:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Domain Scope:} While our study spans three domains, enterprise governance extends into specialized regulatory frameworks (e.g., HIPAA for healthcare, SOX for financial reporting, GDPR for data protection) that impose domain-specific requirements not captured by our benchmark. Generalization to these domains requires future investigation.
    
    \item \textbf{Scalar Arithmetic Reasoning:} Small free-tier LLMs (7--8B parameters) consistently struggle with strict numerical inequality boundaries. This limitation motivates the hybrid architecture (System C) and suggests that retrieval-grounded governance should be paired with deterministic numerical pre-filters in production.
    
    \item \textbf{Inference Infrastructure:} Cloud free-tier rate limits (approximately 10--20 requests/minute on OpenRouter free-tagged models) restrict evaluation throughput and directly impacted System B's standalone benchmark performance. Production deployments would require dedicated on-premise inference engines (e.g., vLLM, TGI) or paid API tiers to achieve unthrottled throughput.
    
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Implementation Quality:} The fairness of our comparison depends on the quality of System A's implementation. While we invested substantial effort in building a competent baseline (47--61 conditional branches per domain), a different engineer might produce a more or less complete rule set. The rule implementation itself is a source of variance that we cannot fully control.
    
    \item \textbf{Test Set Scale:} With 30 scenarios per domain ($N=90$ total) and 30 policy-change scenarios, our test sets are sufficient for paired statistical tests but too small for fine-grained per-category analysis. Larger test sets would enable tighter confidence intervals and more robust subgroup analyses.
    
    \item \textbf{Single Annotator:} Gold labels and human validation were performed by a single annotator. Multi-annotator labeling with inter-annotator agreement metrics would strengthen the reliability of the evaluation ground truth.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
This study presents the first rigorous, multi-domain empirical evaluation of retrieval-grounded governance versus hand-encoded rule-based baselines for LLM multi-agent systems. Our central finding is a fundamental trade-off: while hand-encoded rule engines excel at static numerical boundaries (achieving up to 100\% accuracy on deterministic thresholds), they suffer severe and \emph{silent} failures under policy drift---a 70.0\% stale-code error rate when policy documents are updated without corresponding code changes.

Retrieval-grounded governance eliminates policy-code drift by dynamically grounding governance decisions in updated policy documents with zero code modifications. In our headline robustness experiment, retrieval-grounded governance achieved 76.7\% accuracy post-edit versus 30.0\% for static rules ($p = 0.00131$, McNemar paired test), with the performance gap surviving Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Based on these findings, we recommend a \textbf{hybrid tiered governance architecture} for production deployment: fast deterministic rule filters for numerical threshold checks (Tier 1), combined with retrieval-grounded semantic judges for qualitative, contextual, and exception-based governance decisions (Tier 2). This architecture captures the latency and reliability advantages of rule-based systems while maintaining the adaptability and traceability benefits of retrieval-grounded governance.

All code, data, evaluation scenarios, and statistical analysis scripts are released as open source to support reproducibility and future research.\footnote{Repository: \url{https://github.com/amano2/GovRe}}
